Regions of interest (ROI)

A ROI is the small patch of the photo that REDGIM actually reads. Everything else in the image is ignored.

That matters more than it sounds: the whole analysis comes out of the average colour inside those little boxes. A ROI in the wrong place is a wrong result, however good the maths that follows.

What makes a good ROI​

A good ROI is homogeneous: the colour inside it is uniform. A ROI that catches half sample and half background mixes two colours and returns an average that exists nowhere.

In practice, three rules cover most cases:

  1. Stay away from edges. Edges have shadow, reflection and transition.
  2. Prefer large over small - as long as it stays uniform. More pixels, less noise.
  3. Use the same size on every sample. A fair comparison requires a comparable measurement.

How the app measures homogeneity​

REDGIM can assess on its own whether a ROI is good. It builds the colour histogram of the box across seven channels (R, G, B, grey, hue, saturation and value) and looks at the shape of each one.

The reasoning is simple: in a uniform region the histogram has a single, narrow peak. In a region mixing sample and background, two peaks or a broad smear appear.

The quality index combines those two things, channel by channel:

Where sss says how well defined the main peak's shape is and npeaksn_{\text{peaks}}npeaks​ is how many peaks showed up. The final index is the mean over the valid channels:

The closer to 1, the more homogeneous the region. That is the number shown in the quality bar next to each ROI.

The ROI optimiser​

Marking dozens of ROIs by hand is tiring and inconsistent. The optimiser does it for you: starting from a point, it grows the box and measures quality at each step, stopping when homogeneity drops below the threshold you set.

Each candidate's score is the sum of the qualities along the growth:

reward=∑k=0gQkQˉ=rewardg+1\text{reward} = \sum_{k=0}^{g} Q_k \qquad \bar{Q} = \frac{\text{reward}}{g + 1}reward=k=0∑g​Qk​Qˉ​=g+1reward​

Summing instead of just looking at the final value has a reason: a region that manages to grow a lot while staying homogeneous accumulates more points than a tiny perfect one or a large mediocre one. That is exactly the balance we want - large area and uniform colour.

The default cut-off is 0.950.950.95, adjustable between 0.700.700.70 and 0.9990.9990.999:

  • High threshold (close to 1) → smaller, extremely uniform ROIs.
  • Low threshold → larger ROIs, tolerating some internal variation.
The size is global

In the end, every ROI gets the same size. That is deliberate: comparing samples measured over different areas introduces a bias that cannot be corrected afterwards.

White reference​

If you photograph at two different moments, the light is never exactly the same - and the measured colour shifts without the chemistry having changed at all.

The white reference is a ROI marked on a region that ought to be white (clean paper, the plate's background). It acts as a ruler: the app uses the colour read there to recalibrate the other ROIs in the same image.

corrected colour=measured colourwhite colour⋅reference white\text{corrected colour} = \frac{\text{measured colour}}{\text{white colour}} \cdot \text{reference white}corrected colour=white colourmeasured colour​⋅reference white

With that, a photo taken darker than another is brought back onto the same scale. It is what makes comparing images from different sessions possible - but it works no miracles: coloured light, partial shadow and overexposure still ruin the measurement.

A clipped pixel does not come back

If a region turned pure white in the photo (255 across all three channels), the information was lost at capture. No correction recovers it. Check the exposure before analysing.
