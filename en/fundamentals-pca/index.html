PCA — Principal Component Analysis

PCA in REDGIM's Comparative Mode

This chapter details the mathematical and geometric principles underpinning REDGIM's Comparative Mode. The development starts directly from real digital image data (color channel intensities measured in regions of interest), demonstrating step-by-step the derivation of the covariance matrix, eigenvalues, eigenvectors, channel weights (loadings), and orthogonal projection, accompanied by an interactive visual laboratory.

1. What is PCA in the Context of Digital Images?​

PCA stands for Principal Component Analysis.

The core objective is:

Reduce the number of variables in a set of sample images while retaining as much of the original color variance and dispersion as possible.

In digital images captured by REDGIM, each sample (or cuvette) is characterized by multiple chromatic channels (Red, Green, and Blue). Frequently, these channels exhibit strong correlation — for instance, when a colorimetric reaction absorbs light across specific visible wavelengths.

PCA transforms the original channels into a new set of uncorrelated variables known as principal components:

  1. The first component (PC1) points in the direction where image data presents the maximum possible variance;
  2. The second component (PC2) points in the direction of the greatest remaining variance, strictly perpendicular (orthogonal) to the first;
  3. Subsequent components (PC3, ...) successively follow the same principle, being mutually orthogonal.

Physical Meaning

The Red channel varies more than the Green channel (Var⁡(R)=17>Var⁡(G)=8\operatorname{Var}(R) = 17 > \operatorname{Var}(G) = 8Var(R)=17>Var(G)=8), reflecting that the reaction's optical absorbance creates stronger contrast in Red. The positive covariance (+6+6+6) indicates that samples that are darker in Red also tend to be darker in Green.

4. The Spectral Problem: Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors​

To find the optimal principal directions, we solve:

Cv=λvC v = \lambda vCv=λv

where CCC is the 2×22 \times 22×2 covariance matrix, vvv is the eigenvector (direction of the principal axis), and λ\lambdaλ is the eigenvalue (magnitude of variance along that axis).

5. Algebraic Solution for Eigenvalues​

Setting the determinant to zero:

det⁡(C−λI)=0\det(C - \lambda I) = 0det(C−λI)=0 ∣17−λ668−λ∣=0\begin{vmatrix} 17 - \lambda & 6 \\ 6 & 8 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} = 0​17−λ6​68−λ​​=0 (17−λ)(8−λ)−36=0  ⟹  λ2−25λ+100=0(17 - \lambda)(8 - \lambda) - 36 = 0 \implies \lambda^2 - 25\lambda + 100 = 0(17−λ)(8−λ)−36=0⟹λ2−25λ+100=0

Factoring:

(λ−20)(λ−5)=0(\lambda - 20)(\lambda - 5) = 0(λ−20)(λ−5)=0

Yielding integer eigenvalues:

λ1=20andλ2=5\boxed{\lambda_1 = 20} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \boxed{\lambda_2 = 5}λ1​=20​andλ2​=5​ PC1⟷λ1=20PC2⟷λ2=5\boxed{PC1 \longleftrightarrow \lambda_1 = 20} \qquad \boxed{PC2 \longleftrightarrow \lambda_2 = 5}PC1⟷λ1​=20​PC2⟷λ2​=5​

With v2=1v_2 = 1v2​=1, we obtain v=[21]v = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}v=[21​]. The Euclidean norm is ∥v∥=22+12=5\|v\| = \sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{5}∥v∥=22+12​=5​. Normalizing to unit length:

What Channel Loadings Reveal

In eigenvector v^1\hat{v}_1v^1​:

  • The weight of the Red Channel (RRR) is 25≈0.894\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \approx \mathbf{0.894}5​2​≈0.894;

The Red channel contributes twice as much influence as the Green channel to PC1! This is the diagnostic information REDGIM's loadings plot delivers: identifying which optical channel drives sample discrimination.
