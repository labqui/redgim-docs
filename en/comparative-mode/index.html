Comparative Mode - "which ones look alike?"

Here the question changes. You don't want a number: you want to know who goes with whom.

No sample needs a known value. The app compares the intensity distribution of the regions of interest across all of them and shows the groupings that emerge naturally - if any do.

The data is not the average colour: it is the histogram​

This is the point that sets Comparative Mode apart from Prediction Mode.

Each region of interest does not enter the calculation as a single colour. It enters as its histogram - the full distribution of intensities, 256 bins per channel, the three channels adding up to 768 numbers per ROI.

Comparing samples is therefore a comparison of histogram similarity. Two regions are alike when they distribute their pixels in a similar way, which is far richer information than the average.

Why this matters

A region that is half light and half dark has the same average as a uniformly grey region. By average colour they would be identical. By histogram they are obviously different: one has two peaks, the other has one.

That is why Comparative Mode works on samples with no uniform colour - texture, gradient and phase mixing stay preserved in the data.

The problem: too much information​

768 numbers per ROI is too much to compare by eye. PCA (principal component analysis) solves this by finding the few directions that actually matter.

What PCA does​

Picture your samples as points scattered in space. PCA looks for the direction in which the points are most spread out - that becomes the first principal component, PC1. Then it looks for the second most spread out direction among those left, and so on.

The trick is that "most spread out" means "where the samples differ most". So looking at PC1 and PC2 is usually enough to see the whole structure.

Formally, the centred data matrix is decomposed as:

X=TP⊤+E\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{P}^{\top} + \mathbf{E}X=TP⊤+E
  • T\mathbf{T}T are the scores - each sample's position in the new axis system.
  • P\mathbf{P}P are the loadings - how much each original colour channel contributed to each component.
  • E\mathbf{E}E is the remainder, what the chosen components could not represent.

The directions come from the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix:

C=1n−1X⊤XCpk=λkpk\mathbf{C} = \frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X} \qquad \mathbf{C}\mathbf{p}_k = \lambda_k \mathbf{p}_kC=n−11​X⊤XCpk​=λk​pk​

Each eigenvalue λk\lambda_kλk​ tells how much variation that component carries. The fraction it explains is simply its share of the total:

explained variancek=λk∑jλj\text{explained variance}_k = \frac{\lambda_k}{\sum_j \lambda_j}explained variancek​=∑j​λj​λk​​
Centring is not a detail

Before anything else, the app subtracts each channel's mean. Without that, PC1 would point at the data's "centre of mass" instead of pointing where it varies - and the plot would say nothing.

What comes out of it​

Three plots, each answering a different question: where the samples ended up (scores), why they went there (loadings) and how much of the picture you are seeing (explained variance).

→ How to read each of them

What PCA does not do​

It doesn't tell you what each group is. It shows there are two clusters; saying one is "genuine" and the other "counterfeit" is your call, knowing what you put in there.

It doesn't prove causation. If one group's samples were photographed on one day and the other group's on another day, PCA will separate them beautifully - by light, not by chemistry. That is the most common mistake and the hardest to notice afterwards.

There is no absolute right or wrong. Unlike Prediction Mode, there is no true value here to measure error against. Validation is you being able to explain why the groups landed where they did.
