Comparative and Classification Mode - "which look alike and which class do they belong to?"

Here the question changes. You don't want to estimate a continuous numerical value: you want to know who goes with whom or whether samples reliably belong to known classes.

The app compares the intensity distribution (histograms) of the regions of interest and provides two pathways: unsupervised exploration of natural clusters (PCA) or supervised classification by classes (PLS-DA).

Mathematical Foundations & Interactive Lab

To understand in depth how PCA decomposes the covariance matrix, solves for eigenvalues/eigenvectors, and performs orthogonal data rotation and projection, see the dedicated didactic guide: PCA — Principal Component Analysis, complete with an interactive visual lab.

The data is not the average colour: it is the histogram​

This is the point that sets Comparative and Classification Mode apart from Prediction Mode.

Each region of interest does not enter the calculation as a single colour. It enters as its histogram - the full distribution of intensities, 256 bins per channel, the three channels adding up to 768 numbers per ROI.

Comparing samples is therefore a comparison of histogram similarity. Two regions are alike when they distribute their pixels in a similar way, which is far richer information than the average.

Why this matters

A region that is half light and half dark has the same average as a uniformly grey region. By average colour they would be identical. By histogram they are obviously different: one has two peaks, the other has one.

That is why the method works on samples with no uniform colour - texture, gradient and phase mixing stay preserved in the data.

Identifying samples within groups: Group A1, A2…​

When configuring ROIs in Comparative or Classification Mode, each region is assigned to a class or group (for example, Group A, Group B, Group C...).

To guarantee full traceability when using multiple images or replicates, REDGIM automatically identifies each sample by combining the group letter with the source image index:

  • Group A1: sample belonging to Group A originating from the first image;
  • Group A2: sample belonging to Group A originating from the second image;
  • Group B1, Group B2... and so on.

This indexed code appears in the ROI title during capture, on point labels in the scores plot, and in each row of the results tables and confusion matrix. You can also edit the region at any time to assign a custom text code.

1. PCA: Principal Component Analysis (Unsupervised)​

768 numbers per ROI is too much to compare by eye. PCA solves this by finding the few directions that actually matter in the data space, without prior knowledge of group labels.

What PCA does​

Picture your samples as points scattered in 768-dimensional space. PCA looks for the direction in which the points are most spread out - that becomes the first principal component, PC1. Then it looks for the second most spread out direction among orthogonal ones (PC2), and so on.

The trick is that "most spread out" means "where the samples differ most". So looking at PC1 and PC2 is usually enough to see the whole structure.

Formally, the centred data matrix X\mathbf{X}X is decomposed as:

X=TP⊤+E\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{P}^{\top} + \mathbf{E}X=TP⊤+E
  • T\mathbf{T}T are the scores - each sample's position in the new axis system.
  • P\mathbf{P}P are the loadings - how much each original histogram bin contributed to each component.
  • E\mathbf{E}E is the residual matrix.

The directions come from the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix:

C=1n−1X⊤XCpk=λkpk\mathbf{C} = \frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X} \qquad \mathbf{C}\mathbf{p}_k = \lambda_k \mathbf{p}_kC=n−11​X⊤XCpk​=λk​pk​

Each eigenvalue λk\lambda_kλk​ tells how much variation that component carries:

explained variancek=λk∑jλj\text{explained variance}_k = \frac{\lambda_k}{\sum_j \lambda_j}explained variancek​=∑j​λj​λk​​
Centring is not a detail

Before anything else, the app subtracts each channel's mean. Without that, PC1 would point at the data's "centre of mass" instead of pointing where it varies - and the plot would say nothing.

2. PLS-DA: Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (Supervised)​

When the goal is not merely exploratory, but to statistically classify samples into known groups (e.g., genuine vs. counterfeit, control vs. treated, brand A vs. brand B), PCA may fall short: it searches for the greatest overall variance, which can sometimes stem from secondary experimental variations (like lighting) rather than group differences.

PLS-DA is the supervised adaptation of Partial Least Squares regression for classification tasks.

How PLS-DA works​

  1. Class matrix Y\mathbf{Y}Y: The app constructs a binary indicator matrix (dummy matrix), where each column represents a class (Group A, Group B...). If sample iii belongs to Group A, the corresponding row contains 1 in the Group A column and 0 elsewhere.

  2. Covariance maximisation: The algorithm decomposes the spectral matrix X\mathbf{X}X and the class matrix Y\mathbf{Y}Y simultaneously, finding latent variables (LVs) that maximise the covariance directly between colour/histogram features and the target classes:

    X=TP⊤+EY=TQ⊤+F\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{P}^{\top} + \mathbf{E} \qquad \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{Q}^{\top} + \mathbf{F}X=TP⊤+EY=TQ⊤+F

  3. Classification: For each sample, the model estimates a predicted probability/score vector y^i\hat{\mathbf{y}}_iy^​i​. The sample is assigned to the class with the highest predicted value:

    assigned class=arg⁡max⁡ky^ik\text{assigned class} = \arg\max_k \hat{y}_{ik}assigned class=argkmax​y^​ik​

  4. Cross-validation (LOOCV): The optimal number of latent variables is selected via Leave-One-Out cross-validation, preventing overfitting and ensuring reported accuracy reflects real predictive power.

What PLS-DA provides​

  • Confusion Matrix: Cross-table of Actual vs. Predicted classes, displaying correct assignments and misclassifications.
  • Overall Accuracy: Percentage of correctly classified samples across all groups.
  • Class-specific Figures of Merit: Sensitivity, Specificity, Precision, and F1-Score for each group.
  • VIP Scores (Variable Importance in Projection): Identifies exactly which channels and intensity ranges of the histogram were crucial for discriminating classes (VIP>1.0VIP > 1.0VIP>1.0).

Best practices in comparative and classification analysis​

Do not confuse correlation with causation. If all Group A samples were photographed in the morning and Group B samples in the afternoon, both PCA and PLS-DA may separate them due to shifts in ambient lighting rather than chemical differences. Always photograph under consistent conditions and interleave samples.

Validate with independent samples. A well-fitted PLS-DA model provides high sensitivity and specificity. To confirm real-world robustness, test with samples prepared on different days or across separate batches.
