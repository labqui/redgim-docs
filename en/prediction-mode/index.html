Prediction Mode - "how much is there?"

This is the mode for when you want a number at the end: the nitrite concentration in water, the content of an active ingredient, the pH of a solution.

The idea in one sentence​

You show the app a few samples whose value you already know, it learns the relationship between colour and value, and then applies that relationship to the samples you don't know.

The middle step - "is the curve any good?" - is what separates a trustworthy result from a pretty, wrong number. That is why the metrics section further down deserves attention.

The calibration curve​

In the simplest case, the relationship between colour and concentration is a straight line. The app looks for the line that passes as close as possible to all your standards:

y^=b0+b1x\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 xy^​=b0​+b1​x

Where xxx is the ROI's colour measurement, y^\hat{y}y^​ is the estimated value and b0b_0b0​, b1b_1b1​ are the two numbers that define the line. The method that finds those two numbers is called OLS (least squares). It picks the line that leaves the smallest possible error, adding up the squared errors:

min⁡b0, b1∑i=1n(yi−b0−b1xi)2\min_{b_0,\, b_1} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i \right)^2b0​,b1​min​i=1∑n​(yi​−b0​−b1​xi​)2

Squaring serves two purposes: errors above and below don't cancel out, and one large error weighs disproportionately more than several small ones.

When a line isn't enough

OLS uses one colour channel at a time. If your reaction's colour shifts across several channels at once - or if coloured interferents are shifting it too - a line over a single channel throws information away. That is where PLS comes in.

When colour gets complicated: PLS​

PLS (partial least squares regression) uses every channel at once. Instead of picking a channel, it builds combinations of them - the so-called latent variables - favouring precisely the combinations most related to the value you want to predict.

Think of it this way: instead of asking "which channel explains best?", PLS asks "which mixture of channels explains best?".

X=TP⊤+Ey^=Tq+f\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{P}^{\top} + \mathbf{E} \qquad \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{q} + \mathbf{f}X=TP⊤+Ey^​=Tq+f

In plain words: the colour matrix X\mathbf{X}X is rewritten as a handful of latent variables T\mathbf{T}T (plus a leftover E\mathbf{E}E, which is what didn't fit), and it is from those latent variables that the value y^\hat{\mathbf{y}}y^​ is computed.

How many latent variables to use​

That is the decision in PLS, and the app helps with cross-validation: it hides part of the standards, predicts those hidden ones with a model trained on the rest, and measures the error. It repeats this while varying the number of latent variables.

  • Too few → the model hasn't captured the chemistry; it gets everything wrong.
  • Too many → the model has memorised the noise in your standards. It looks great on them and terrible on any new sample. That is called overfitting.

The right point is where the validation error stops falling. The app shows you that chart so you can choose with your eyes rather than in the dark.

Reading the confidence measures​

After the computation, REDGIM shows a set of numbers. They are not decoration - each answers a different question about how far you can trust the model.

R² - how much the colour explains​

R2=1−∑i(yi−y^i)2∑i(yi−yˉ)2R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i - \bar{y})^2}R2=1−∑i​(yi​−yˉ​)2∑i​(yi​−y^​i​)2​

It runs from 0 to 1 (it can go negative if the model is worse than simply guessing the mean). Close to 1, colour explains nearly all the variation in the values.

A high R² is no guarantee

A high R2R^2R2 obtained with many latent variables can be pure overfitting. Always read it alongside RMSEP, which is measured on samples the model has not seen.

RMSEP - the average error, in your units​

RMSEP=1n∑i=1n(yi−y^i)2\mathrm{RMSEP} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2}RMSEP=n1​i=1∑n​(yi​−y^​i​)2​

This is the most direct measure of all: the typical error of a prediction, in the same unit as your property. If you measure concentration in mg/L, RMSEP comes out in mg/L too. The smaller, the better.

RPD - the error compared to how much the samples vary​

RPD=SDRMSEP\mathrm{RPD} = \frac{\mathrm{SD}}{\mathrm{RMSEP}}RPD=RMSEPSD​

It divides the standard deviation of the true values by the model's error. It answers: "is this error small relative to what I am measuring?".

RPDReading
below 2the model barely tells the samples apart
between 2 and 3good enough for screening
above 3good enough for quantification

Bias - the error that always leans the same way​

bias=1n∑i=1n(yi−y^i)\text{bias} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)bias=n1​i=1∑n​(yi​−y^​i​)

While RMSEP measures the size of the error, bias measures its direction. A bias far from zero says the model errs systematically high or low - a classic symptom of different lighting between the photo of the standards and the photo of the samples.

What usually goes wrong​

Sample outside the standards' range. If your standards run from 0 to 100 and the sample reads 180, the model is extrapolating: using a relationship where it was never verified. The app flags this as high leverage. Treat the number with suspicion.

Lighting changed along the way. This is the number one cause of poor results. Photograph everything in the same session, in the same light box, and use the white reference.

Badly placed ROIs. A ROI that catches an edge, a shadow or paper instead of sample poisons the colour measurement. The page on regions of interest covers this.

Too few standards. With three points any line looks good. Five to seven points well spread across the range is the reasonable minimum.
