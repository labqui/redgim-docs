What is REDGIM?

REDGIM is an app born in 2016 out of one idea: to bring together two worlds that rarely meet.

On one side, everything a mobile device already carries and that became popular in everyone's hands: a good camera, a screen, processing power, digital images anywhere. On the other, multivariate data analysis, historically locked inside benchtop software, expensive licences and a long learning curve. REDGIM exists to join the two.

The experience came before the code​

The whole development started from the experience of the person using it. The app was designed screen by screen before it became code, and the study below is part of that design.

The study comes from an earlier stage of the project, when Prediction Mode was still called Colorimeter Mode, and it is in Portuguese. What survived from it intact were the experience decisions, and they were few and firm:

  • An interface you already know how to use. The gestures are inherited from the apps everybody already has: pinch to zoom, drag and drop items, the way photo handling works on Instagram. It holds for the way in, too: a .gim arriving through WhatsApp or any other messaging app opens straight into REDGIM when tapped.
  • Progressive actions. At most two options at each decision point. The first question is just that: estimate a concentration, or compare small patches of an image.
  • Reprocessing. You can open the app later, unhurried, and keep adjusting and reviewing the results. The analysis does not end when the calculation runs.
  • Sharing analyses and results. What you produce can be taken to a conference, a paper or a research group. The .gim file works as a reference for the reviewer: it gets reopened and assessed, not merely read.
  • Exporting the calculation structure. What the app does internally comes out with it, and can be audited to validate the numbers.

The complexity is hidden, not absent​

The app does not simplify the maths. It simplifies the path to it.

Inside there is validation, consistency checking and a battery of tests. Outside, the screen shows few buttons at a time, and they appear progressively as decisions get made. You do not pick from twenty options: you answer one question, and the next one only shows up because the previous one was answered.

This is an app with a gentle way in. Someone who has never heard of a latent variable can reach a result, and stays guided by the app all the way there.

Two modes, two questions​

In practice REDGIM works in two ways. It is the first choice on the home screen, and it is worth understanding from the start that these are two independent paths, not two stages of the same process.

What the two share is the upper part: the photograph, the marking and the building of the histograms. Below that they do not talk to each other. One ties colour to a quantity and needs samples of known value; the other does not ask what the colour means and needs no values at all.

Prediction Mode​

Puts forward a colorimetric analysis from the digital image. The colour measured in the image is tied to a reference value, and the resulting model estimates that value for new samples.

This is the path to "how much is there".

Comparative Mode​

Here there is no uniform colour to work with. Comparative Mode works by histogram similarity, computed from the regions marked on the image.

Two samples are alike when the distribution of intensities in their regions is alike, which carries far more information than an average colour and works even when the region is not a single colour.

This is the path to "which ones look alike".

Why a histogram, and not an average colour

An average colour throws away everything inside the region: texture, gradient, the presence of two phases. The histogram keeps that structure.

The multivariate methods (PLS, in Prediction Mode, and PCA, in Comparative Mode) work on the whole histogram. The univariate method uses a single point of it: the maximum of the distribution, on the chosen colour channel.

What goes into the calculation​

In both modes, what feeds the model is not the whole photo. You mark small parts of the image, and those are what define the features the calculation uses.

The path is the same in any analysis: navigate the photo itself, zoom in and tap the part that matters. Each mark is stored, and it is the set of them that goes into the model.

Those marks have a name, and it shows up constantly from here on: regions of interest, or ROIs.

What you can do​

In the order things happen:

  1. Bring in the image from the device camera, front or back; from an external camera connected over USB OTG; or by importing an image already on the device or coming from another digital image source.
  2. Mark the regions of interest, navigating and zooming into the photo.
  3. Choose the analysis: estimate a value, or compare samples with each other.
  4. Run it and see the result in the app itself, with histograms, colour channel analysis, PCA, PLS and the charts of each method.
  5. Reprocess the same image as many times as you want, with other parameters, and compare different runs.
  6. Save and share the images, the results and the whole analysis in a .gim file.
