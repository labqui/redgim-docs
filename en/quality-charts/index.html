Quality charts

These charts don't talk about the model - they talk about the image. They are the ones you look at before computing anything, to know whether the input material is any good.

One point is worth insisting on: no method can later fix a colour that was measured wrong. Five minutes here save a whole analysis redone.

ROI histogram​

For each region of interest, how many pixels have each intensity, channel by channel. It is the rawest data in the app - in PLS, it is literally what goes into the calculation.

The axes are not the ones you expect

Anyone coming from spectrophotometry finds this odd, and rightly so. In a spectrum the analytical signal sits on the y axis: the stronger the colour, the higher the absorbance. The histogram does not work that way.

The x axis is the intensity of the colour, from 0 to 255. The y axis is how many pixels have that intensity. Absorption shows up as a horizontal shift: the more the solution absorbs in a channel, the further left that channel's distribution slides.

There is one more inversion underneath: the spectrophotometer measures the light that is absorbed, while the camera records the light that is reflected. Same information, seen from the other side.

The most useful reading rule: on a blank, the R, G and B distributions sit almost on top of each other, because without colour there is only greyscale. When one of them separates from the others, that is where the colour is. Stronger lighting shifts the whole set to the right, and weaker lighting to the left, without separating it.

Reading it in three shapes:

A single narrow peak is what you want: the region has one colour only.

Two peaks means the ROI sits on a boundary - half sample, half background. The average between the two peaks is a number that corresponds to nothing real.

A peak pressed against 255 is overexposure. The pixels there clipped and all turned white: the information was destroyed at the moment of the photo. There is no possible correction - rephotograph with less light or a shorter exposure.

A peak pressed against 0 is the opposite, underexposure, and meets the same fate.

Peak shape​

The app doesn't just draw the histogram: it assesses the shape of the main peak and says whether it is well behaved.

A well-defined peak - narrow and symmetric - indicates uniform colour. A broad, asymmetric or shouldered peak indicates that more than one colour is mixed in there, even if it looks uniform to the eye.

That judgement is made across seven channels (R, G, B, grey, hue, saturation and value) and feeds the quality index described in Regions of interest:

qchannel=0.7⋅s+0.3⋅1npeaksq_{\text{channel}} = 0.7 \cdot s + 0.3 \cdot \frac{1}{n_{\text{peaks}}}qchannel​=0.7⋅s+0.3⋅npeaks​1​

A channel only enters the calculation if its peak has a recognisable shape. If no channel is valid, the app does not risk a number - it says it could not assess.

Homogeneity per ROI​

A bar chart with every ROI side by side and the colour quality of each, from 0 to 1.

It is the app's most efficient chart for finding trouble, because it shows every region at once. A bar visibly lower than its neighbours points straight at the ROI that needs attention.

How to use it: look at the overall profile before the absolute values.

  • All bars high and similar → consistent set, go ahead and compute.
  • One or two low bars → reposition those ROIs and recompute.
  • All bars low → the problem is not the ROI, it is the photo. Uneven lighting, poor focus, or a genuinely heterogeneous sample.
  • Bars falling from left to right → there is a light gradient across the image. The darker part is being measured under different conditions from the lighter part.

That last case is treacherous: the ROIs are right, the photo looks fine to the naked eye, and the results still come out biased. The gradient shows up in this chart long before it shows up to you.

RGB channels​

The three intensity curves overlaid, to compare how each channel behaves in the same region.

It answers a practical question: in which channel does my reaction show up best?

If the reaction's colour goes from colourless to pink, the green channel tends to vary a lot (because pink absorbs green) while red barely moves. In that case, an OLS model on the green channel may be all you need - no PLS, no complication.

The app suggests a channel, but showing all three curves puts the decision out in the open.

The channel that responds is the complementary one. A yellow solution absorbs in the blue range, so it is the blue distribution that travels as the concentration rises, while red and green barely move. As a rule of thumb: look for the channel opposite the colour you see.

When one channel is not enough

This works when the reaction's colour coincides with one of the three channels. Some substances do not behave that way: natural extracts used as pH indicators, for instance, change colour without following a gradient, and each pH produces a colour of its own Terci, D. B. L.; Rossi, A. V. Indicadores naturais de pH: Usar papel ou solução?. Quimica Nova v. 25, n. 4, p. 684-688, 2002.. There no single channel describes the system, and that is the case for using the whole histogram, with PLS or PCA.

CIE 1931 chromaticity diagram​

The horseshoe-shaped colour map, with your ROIs' colours plotted on it.

Unlike RGB, which is how the camera stores colour, CIE 1931 is how human vision perceives colour. Two samples close together on this diagram are colours a person would see as similar.

What it is for in practice: seeing whether your samples trace a coherent path through colour space. In a well-made calibration series, the points draw a trajectory - colour walks in one direction as concentration grows. Points scattered without a trajectory indicate that something other than concentration is moving the colour.

Where this chart shines

When you have two competing reactions or a coloured interferent, the path on the diagram stops being a line and becomes a curve or a fan. That is far more visible here than on a per-channel intensity plot.
