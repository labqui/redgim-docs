Charts and Results: Comparative and Classification

The results and charts displayed depend on the method chosen for the analysis:

  • In PCA (unsupervised): three exploratory plots read together — explained variance (how much of the total you are seeing), scores (where samples landed), and loadings (which channel pulled where).
  • In PLS-DA (supervised): four confirmatory visualisations read together — the latent variable scores (LV1×LV2LV_1 \times LV_2LV1​×LV2​, showcasing group separation in latent space), the estimated class values with decision threshold (y=0.5y = 0.5y=0.5, evaluating samples individually), the confusion matrix and metrics (sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1-score), and VIP scores (which channels and histogram bins discriminate groups).
Unsupervised vs. supervised models

In PCA there are no known target classes or numerical predictions to check: three charts are sufficient to describe the natural variability structure of the data.

In PLS-DA, however, a supervised model is calibrated to discriminate between predefined classes (Group A, Group B...). Consequently, beyond sample distribution, the app validates predictive performance via cross-validation (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score) and reveals the chromatic markers responsible for differentiation using VIP scores.

PCA Results (Unsupervised)​

Explained variance​

Bars with the fraction each principal component carries, plus a line with the running total.

explainedk=λk∑jλjcumulativeK=∑k=1Kexplainedk\text{explained}_k = \frac{\lambda_k}{\sum_j \lambda_j} \qquad \text{cumulative}_K = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \text{explained}_kexplainedk​=∑j​λj​λk​​cumulativeK​=k=1∑K​explainedk​

How to decide how many components to look at: find where the cumulative line flattens. If PC1 and PC2 add up to 92% and PC3 adds 3%, there is no reason to go past the second - the scores plot on PC1×PC2 already shows nearly everything.

Cumulative at PC1+PC2What it means
above 85%the scores plot tells almost the whole story
between 60% and 85%read carefully; look at PC3 as well
below 60%the information is spread out; conclusions about groups are fragile

The app lets you swap which components go on the axes precisely to investigate that last case.

Scores plot​

The map of the samples. Each point is a sample, and distance is colour difference.

If you tagged groups while configuring the ROIs, the points come coloured by group - and then the reading is immediate.

What to look for:

  • Groups apart, no overlap → there is a systematic colour difference between them.
  • Overlapping groups → by colour, these samples are the same thing.
  • One group spread out, another compact → the spread one has more internal variation; it may be genuine sample heterogeneity or inconsistent preparation.
  • Separation along PC1 → the largest source of variation in the set is exactly what distinguishes the groups. That is the ideal case.
  • Separation only on PC2 or PC3 → something bigger is varying that is not your grouping. Nearly always lighting or material batch. Worth investigating before celebrating.

The plot includes a 95% Hotelling ellipse: the region where 95% of the samples would fall if they all came from the same set.

(x−xˉ)⊤S−1(x−xˉ)≤χ0.95; 22(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) \leq \chi^{2}_{0.95;\,2}(x−xˉ)⊤S−1(x−xˉ)≤χ0.95;22​

It comes out tilted when the two components vary together, and points outside it are outlier candidates.

Outside the ellipse is not a verdict

Before discarding a sample, open its photo and its ROI. In practice, most points outside the ellipse are badly placed ROIs - catching an edge, a shadow or a reflection - rather than chemically different samples.

Loadings plot​

While the scores show where the samples ended up, the loadings show on account of what. One plot per component.

Each point is an original colour channel, and its position tells how much that channel contributed to the component.

How to use it in practice: find on the scores plot the direction that separates your groups, then check on the loadings which channels dominate that direction. If the groups separate along PC1 and PC1 is dominated by the red channel, red is what distinguishes the groups - and that has to make sense with the chemistry of the reaction.

When it doesn't, that is a warning sign. Separation driven by a channel the reaction should not affect is usually a lighting artefact.

A classic mistake​

The scores plot is too convincing. Two well-separated groups on screen give a strong sense of discovery - and that is exactly where the risk lies.

PCA separates on any systematic colour difference. If group A's samples were photographed on Monday and group B's on Thursday, with slightly different light, the separation will look beautiful and have nothing to do with chemistry.

The defence is simple and not statistical: photograph everything in the same session, under the same lighting, and interleave the groups in the shooting order. If they still separate, the separation is real.

Watch it happen​

The map below is the scores chart, with the PC1 loadings beside it. The maths is the app's own: the R, G and B means of each ROI, centred, and the principal components of the covariance matrix. Click a sample to see its three channels and where it landed.

The two groups differ by two independent things, and each has its own control: chemistry, which moves along a colour direction (red falls and green rises, as in a reaction that consumes the coloured species), and lighting, which moves along the grey axis, shifting all three channels together.

Interactive · use the controls
● Group A● Group BExplained PC1 71.0%PC2 18.5%PC1+PC2 89.5%

Click a sample to see its R, G and B, and where it landed on the map.

Three things to try:

  1. Zero the lighting and raise the chemistry. The groups move apart along PC1, which now carries 91% of the variation, and the loadings show red and green in opposite directions, with blue near zero - blue takes no part in the reaction, and PC1 says so. This is the good case, and it is the one worth comparing the other two against.
  2. Now zero the chemistry and raise only the lighting. The separation comes back, as clean as before, with no chemical difference between the groups at all. It is the classic mistake happening in front of you. Look at the loadings: all three channels in the same direction and of similar weight - the grey axis. What changed was brightness, and brightness is not chemistry. The tool says so when that signature shows up.
  3. Put the chemistry back at maximum and raise the spread. The groups merge on screen, and the cumulative figure barely moves: it goes from 97% down to around 83%, a small drop for so large a loss. It measures how much of the total scatter the two components carry, and it knows nothing about groups - a high number there was never a promise of separation.

PLS-DA Results (Supervised Classification)​

In PLS-DA, the goal is to evaluate whether samples genuinely belong to their designated classes and to understand the physicochemical basis of discrimination.

Latent Variable Scores Plot (LV1×LV2LV_1 \times LV_2LV1​×LV2​)​

The PLS-DA scores plot maps samples onto the two-dimensional subspace defined by the first two latent variables (LV1LV_1LV1​ and LV2LV_2LV2​).

Unlike PCA — where projection solely maximises instrumental variance in X\mathbf{X}X without prior knowledge of labels —, the PLS algorithm simultaneously maximises the covariance between instrumental descriptor data (X\mathbf{X}X, RGB histograms) and the dummy class matrix (Y\mathbf{Y}Y).

  • Plot Axes:
    • XXX-axis: First latent variable (LV1LV_1LV1​), accompanied by explained instrumental variance (RX2R^2_XRX2​) and explained class variance (RY2R^2_YRY2​).
    • YYY-axis: Second latent variable (LV2LV_2LV2​), also accompanied by RX2R^2_XRX2​ and RY2R^2_YRY2​.
  • Points and Colours: Every sample is plotted with the distinctive color and marker of its true group (Group A, Group B, etc.).
  • Interpretation: If the supervised model successfully captures class differences, the groups appear distinctly clustered and segregated across the LV1×LV2LV_1 \times LV_2LV1​×LV2​ plane, verifying that the chosen latent variables isolate genuine chemical differences.

Estimated Class Values Plot (Decision Threshold y=0.5y = 0.5y=0.5)​

Following chemometric classification standards established in analytical literature (Filete et al., 2025), REDGIM provides the estimated class values plot (y^\hat{y}y^​) for each modelled category.

In PLS-DA dummy regression, the target response is binary (y=1y = 1y=1 for samples of the target class, y=0y = 0y=0 for non-target samples). During prediction, the model outputs a continuous value y^\hat{y}y^​ for each sample:

      |                                              |
      |        ▲ [Group B - TN]       ■ [Group C - TN]|  does not belong to class
  0.0 +----------------------------------------------+
      1       2       3       4       5 ...          N   (Samples)
  • Horizontal Axis (XXX): Ordered sample indices (1,2,…,N1, 2, \dots, N1,2,…,N).
  • Vertical Axis (YYY): Continuous predicted class value (y^\hat{y}y^​) for the selected category.
  • Decision Threshold Line (y=0.5y = 0.5y=0.5): The theoretical cut-off dividing class membership:
    • y^≥0.5  ⟹  \hat{y} \ge 0.5 \impliesy^​≥0.5⟹ Sample is predicted to belong to the target class.
    • y^<0.5  ⟹  \hat{y} < 0.5 \impliesy^​<0.5⟹ Sample is predicted to belong to an alternative class.
  • Visual Error Diagnostic: Because every point retains the marker shape and color of its actual group:
    • True Positives (TP): Target class samples plotted above y=0.5y = 0.5y=0.5.
    • False Negatives (FN): Target class samples falling below y=0.5y = 0.5y=0.5 (classification miss).
    • False Positives (FP): Non-target class samples rising above y=0.5y = 0.5y=0.5 (false alarm).
    • True Negatives (TN): Non-target class samples plotted comfortably below y=0.5y = 0.5y=0.5.
  • Class Selector & Live Diagnostic Badge: Interactive filter chips enable switching between groups (Group A, Group B, etc.) with instant live statistics for Sensitivity, Specificity, and TP counts.

Confusion Matrix​

The confusion matrix compares the actual class of each sample (Actual, rows) with the class assigned by the PLS-DA model (Predicted, columns).

  • Main diagonal (in green): Correctly classified samples. For example, a Group A sample predicted as Group A.
  • Off-diagonal (in red): Misclassifications. These indicate instances where a sample from one class was erroneously assigned to another by the model.
  • Overall Accuracy: Displayed at the top of the card, representing the overall percentage of correct predictions across all samples:
Overall Accuracy=∑k=1KCkkNtotal×100%\text{Overall Accuracy} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} C_{kk}}{N_{\text{total}}} \times 100\%Overall Accuracy=Ntotal​∑k=1K​Ckk​​×100%

Where CkkC_{kk}Ckk​ denotes correct assignments along the main diagonal for class kkk, and NtotalN_{\text{total}}Ntotal​ is the total number of evaluated samples.

What to look for in the Confusion Matrix

A matrix where counts fall solely along the main diagonal indicates perfect separation and classification (100% accuracy). Off-diagonal numbers pinpoint spectral overlap or chemical ambiguity between specific classes.

Class-Specific Figures of Merit​

High overall accuracy can mask weaknesses when group sizes are imbalanced. REDGIM therefore computes dedicated metrics for each class on a One-vs-Rest basis:

Sensitivity (Recall)=TPTP+FNSpecificity=TNTN+FPPrecision=TPTP+FPF1-Score=2⋅Precision⋅SensitivityPrecision+Sensitivity\begin{aligned} \text{Sensitivity (Recall)} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \\[8pt] \text{Specificity} &= \frac{TN}{TN + FP} \\[8pt] \text{Precision} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \\[8pt] \text{F1-Score} &= 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Sensitivity}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Sensitivity}} \end{aligned}Sensitivity (Recall)SpecificityPrecisionF1-Score​=TP+FNTP​=TN+FPTN​=TP+FPTP​=2⋅Precision+SensitivityPrecision⋅Sensitivity​​
  • Sensitivity (True Positive Rate): Out of all samples that truly belong to this group, what fraction did the model correctly identify? Low sensitivity indicates false negatives.
  • Specificity (True Negative Rate): Out of all samples belonging to other groups, how many did the model correctly reject? High specificity guarantees that foreign samples are not incorrectly attributed to this class (low false alarm rate).
  • Precision: When the model classifies a sample as belonging to this group, what is the probability that this prediction is correct?
  • F1-Score: Harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity. It serves as the most reliable indicator when balancing errors in groups with few replicates.

VIP Scores (Variable Importance in Projection)​

While the confusion matrix tells you whether the model classified correctly, the VIP (Variable Importance in Projection) plot reveals why separation occurred, highlighting which intensity bins across the RGB histogram were decisive in distinguishing classes.

The VIP score synthesises the influence of each of the 768 spectral variables (256 bins for each of the R, G, and B channels) weighted by the explained variance of the classes across each selected latent variable:

VIPj=p∑a=1A(SSaSStotal)waj2\text{VIP}_j = \sqrt{p \sum_{a=1}^{A} \left( \frac{SS_a}{SS_{\text{total}}} \right) w_{aj}^2}VIPj​=pa=1∑A​(SStotal​SSa​​)waj2​​

Where:

  • p=768p = 768p=768 (total variables across R, G, and B channels);
  • AAA is the number of latent variables selected via cross-validation;
  • SSaSS_aSSa​ is the sum of squares of the class matrix Y\mathbf{Y}Y explained by latent component aaa;
  • SStotalSS_{\text{total}}SStotal​ is the total sum of squares explained in Y\mathbf{Y}Y;
  • wajw_{aj}waj​ is the loading weight of variable jjj in component aaa.

How to interpret the VIP plot​

The plot overlays the curves for the Red, Green, and Blue channels across all pixel intensity levels (0 to 255), including a horizontal benchmark line at 1.01.01.0:

  • VIP>1.0\text{VIP} > 1.0VIP>1.0 (High Relevance): Variables with VIP scores exceeding 1.0 have above-average influence. These represent key spectral biomarkers distinguishing the groups chemically.
  • 0.8≤VIP≤1.00.8 \le \text{VIP} \le 1.00.8≤VIP≤1.0: Moderately influential variables.
  • VIP<0.8\text{VIP} < 0.8VIP<0.8 (Low Relevance): Histogram regions contributing little to discrimination, reflecting background noise or uninformative variance.

If a prominent VIP peak (>1.5> 1.5>1.5) appears within a specific intensity range of the Green channel, for example, the analyst can confidently deduce that colorimetric changes in that spectral zone drive the group separation in the assay.
