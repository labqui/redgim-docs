Comparative Mode charts

There are three charts, and they are read together: one says where the samples landed, another says why, and the third says how much of the picture you are seeing.

Start with explained variance. Without knowing how much of the total the first two components carry, any reading of the scores plot is guesswork.

Why three charts here and eight in Prediction Mode

It is not missing documentation: it is the method. Prediction fits a model, so it has to prove the model works - calibration curve, residuals, error metrics, confidence band, the choice of how many latent variables. Each of those charts answers "can I trust this number?".

Comparative mode estimates no number. It reorganises what is already there to show which samples resemble which, and there is no prediction to check against a known value. With no model to validate, there is nothing to validate - and three charts are enough to read what it has to say.

How to decide how many components to look at: find where the cumulative line flattens. If PC1 and PC2 add up to 92% and PC3 adds 3%, there is no reason to go past the second - the scores plot on PC1×PC2 already shows nearly everything.

The app lets you swap which components go on the axes precisely to investigate that last case.

Scores plot​

The map of the samples. Each point is a sample, and distance is colour difference.

If you tagged groups while configuring the ROIs, the points come coloured by group - and then the reading is immediate.

What to look for:

  • Groups apart, no overlap → there is a systematic colour difference between them.
  • Overlapping groups → by colour, these samples are the same thing.
  • One group spread out, another compact → the spread one has more internal variation; it may be genuine sample heterogeneity or inconsistent preparation.
  • Separation along PC1 → the largest source of variation in the set is exactly what distinguishes the groups. That is the ideal case.
  • Separation only on PC2 or PC3 → something bigger is varying that is not your grouping. Nearly always lighting or material batch. Worth investigating before celebrating.

The plot includes a 95% Hotelling ellipse: the region where 95% of the samples would fall if they all came from the same set.

(x−xˉ)⊤S−1(x−xˉ)≤χ0.95; 22(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) \leq \chi^{2}_{0.95;\,2}(x−xˉ)⊤S−1(x−xˉ)≤χ0.95;22​

It comes out tilted when the two components vary together, and points outside it are outlier candidates.

Outside the ellipse is not a verdict

Before discarding a sample, open its photo and its ROI. In practice, most points outside the ellipse are badly placed ROIs - catching an edge, a shadow or a reflection - rather than chemically different samples.

Loadings plot​

While the scores show where the samples ended up, the loadings show on account of what. One plot per component.

Each point is an original colour channel, and its position tells how much that channel contributed to the component.

How to use it in practice: find on the scores plot the direction that separates your groups, then check on the loadings which channels dominate that direction. If the groups separate along PC1 and PC1 is dominated by the red channel, red is what distinguishes the groups - and that has to make sense with the chemistry of the reaction.

When it doesn't, that is a warning sign. Separation driven by a channel the reaction should not affect is usually a lighting artefact.

A classic mistake​

The scores plot is too convincing. Two well-separated groups on screen give a strong sense of discovery - and that is exactly where the risk lies.

PCA separates on any systematic colour difference. If group A's samples were photographed on Monday and group B's on Thursday, with slightly different light, the separation will look beautiful and have nothing to do with chemistry.

The defence is simple and not statistical: photograph everything in the same session, under the same lighting, and interleave the groups in the shooting order. If they still separate, the separation is real.

Watch it happen​

The map below is the scores chart, with the PC1 loadings beside it. The maths is the app's own: the R, G and B means of each ROI, centred, and the principal components of the covariance matrix. Click a sample to see its three channels and where it landed.

The two groups differ by two independent things, and each has its own control: chemistry, which moves along a colour direction (red falls and green rises, as in a reaction that consumes the coloured species), and lighting, which moves along the grey axis, shifting all three channels together.

Three things to try:

  1. Zero the lighting and raise the chemistry. The groups move apart along PC1, which now carries 91% of the variation, and the loadings show red and green in opposite directions, with blue near zero - blue takes no part in the reaction, and PC1 says so. This is the good case, and it is the one worth comparing the other two against.
  2. Now zero the chemistry and raise only the lighting. The separation comes back, as clean as before, with no chemical difference between the groups at all. It is the classic mistake happening in front of you. Look at the loadings: all three channels in the same direction and of similar weight - the grey axis. What changed was brightness, and brightness is not chemistry. The tool says so when that signature shows up.
  3. Put the chemistry back at maximum and raise the spread. The groups merge on screen, and the cumulative figure barely moves: it goes from 97% down to around 83%, a small drop for so large a loss. It measures how much of the total scatter the two components carry, and it knows nothing about groups - a high number there was never a promise of separation.
