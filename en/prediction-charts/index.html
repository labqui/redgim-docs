Prediction Mode charts

Once the computation runs, REDGIM opens a sequence of charts. None of them is decoration: each answers a different question about the model, and together they tell you whether the result deserves trust.

This page is a reading guide. The order here is the order worth looking at them in.

Calibration curve​

The figure above is the app screen. What follows explains what it shows.

The first and most important one. The horizontal axis holds the property you want to measure

  • concentration, typically. The vertical axis holds the colour signal read from the ROI.

Three things are overlaid:

  • The line - the fitted model.
  • Squares - the standards, the ones whose value you already knew.
  • Circles - the unknown samples, placed at the value the model estimated.

What to look for: the squares should sit on the line, with no curvature. If they draw an arc instead of a straight line, the relationship between colour and concentration is not linear over that range - usually because the range is too wide and colour saturated at the top. The fix is to narrow the range or switch to PLS.

Circles outside the range of the squares are extrapolation: the model is opining about a region where no standard ever taught it. The app flags those samples as high leverage.

Residuals​

A residual is how much the model missed at that particular point:

ei=yi−y^ie_i = y_i - \hat{y}_iei​=yi​−y^​i​

The chart puts the measured value on the horizontal axis and the residual on the vertical one, with a horizontal line at zero for reference.

What to look for: a cloud of points scattered randomly around zero, with no shape at all. That means the model captured everything there was to capture and what remains is noise.

What is a problem:

Residual shapeWhat it means
U shape (or inverted U)the relationship is not linear; a line won't do
Funnel opening to the rightthe error grows with concentration
Nearly all above or below zerothere is bias - measurement conditions changed
One point far from the otherspossible preparation or ROI error in that sample

This chart usually exposes trouble before any metric does. An R2R^2R2 of 0.99 with U-shaped residuals is still a wrong model.

Watch it happen​

The two charts below are the same pair the app shows, and they are linked: clicking a point highlights the same point in both. On the calibration curve you can drag a standard up and down, and the fit redoes itself on the spot - the line, the residuals and the metrics.

Standards are squares and the sample is the circle, as in the app. The estimated concentration is where the sample's signal meets the line.

Interactive · use the controls
y = 23.5 + 192.4 xR² 0.9938RMSEC 4.45Estimated concentration 0.502

Click a point to follow it in both charts. Drag it up or down and the fit redoes itself on the spot.

Four things to try:

  1. Drag a standard off the line. That point's residual grows at once, and the whole line tilts a little towards it. A single wrong point contaminates every sample, not just itself.
  2. Zero the noise and set curvature to half. Curvature is colour saturating at the top of the range: the signal grows less and less. With no noise in the way, the result is the very experiment the paragraph before describes, now run by you: the residuals draw a perfect arc - the inverted U from the table - and R2R^2R2 reads 0.9949. Excellent by any criterion, and the model is still wrong. Take curvature to the maximum and the arc doubles while R2R^2R2 is still 0.9517.
  3. Raise the measurement noise. R2R^2R2 falls and RMSEC rises together, and the residuals grow without forming any shape: scatter is not the same problem as curvature.
  4. Push the sample's signal to the extremes. The circle walks along the line until it leaves the range of the standards - and then the app starts flagging high leverage, because no standard taught the model in that region.

Lowering the number of standards makes all of this more dramatic: with three points, one of them out of place is already enough for the model to lie while looking good.

Confidence interval​

Over the calibration curve the app draws a band around the line: the region where the true value probably lies, with 95% confidence.

The band is not of constant width. It is narrowest in the middle of the calibration range and opens up at the ends - because in the centre there are standards on both sides holding the estimate up, and at the edges there are not.

Practical consequence

Measuring in the middle of your standards' range gives a more precise result than measuring at an end. If the sample you care about tends to fall near an extreme, it is worth redistributing the standards to centre the range on it.

For PLS the calculation follows the ASTM E1655-05 standard; for OLS the app shows the prediction interval. And there is a condition: with all latent variables active there are no degrees of freedom left to estimate the interval. That is why the app asks you to optimise the model before it shows the band.

Latent variable optimisation (PLS)​

A PLS-only chart, and the home of the method's most important decision.

The horizontal axis holds the number of latent variables. The vertical axis holds RMSECV

  • the error measured by cross-validation, that is, measured on samples the model did not use to learn.

What to look for: the curve falls, reaches a minimum, then rises again or flattens out.

  • Before the minimum - the model hasn't captured all the chemistry yet.
  • At the minimum - the point to choose.
  • After the minimum - the model has started memorising noise. Overfitting.

When torn between two similar points, pick the smaller number of variables. A simpler model generalises better, and is easier to defend.

VIP - which colour ranges matter​

Variable Importance in Projection. Three curves, one per channel (R, G and B). Intensity from 0 to 255 on the horizontal axis; on the vertical, how much that intensity range weighed in the model.

VIPj=p∑a=1A[SSa(wja/∥wa∥)2]∑a=1ASSa\mathrm{VIP}_j = \sqrt{ \frac{p \sum_{a=1}^{A} \left[ \mathrm{SS}_a \left( w_{ja} / \lVert \mathbf{w}_a \rVert \right)^2 \right]}{\sum_{a=1}^{A} \mathrm{SS}_a} }VIPj​=∑a=1A​SSa​p∑a=1A​[SSa​(wja​/∥wa​∥)2]​​

Reading it is far simpler than the formula: VIP above 1 means an important variable. That is the established rule of thumb, and it follows from the fact that the mean of the squared VIPs is always 1.

What it is for: if the VIP peaks sit in the green channel, it is the variation in green carrying the information - which usually matches the colour of the reaction. When VIP points at a channel that makes no chemical sense, be suspicious: it may be the lighting, not the sample.

Regression coefficients​

Same layout as VIP - three curves per channel, intensity on the horizontal axis - but with a line at zero.

The difference is that VIP shows importance (always positive) while the coefficient shows direction: above zero, that intensity range pushes the prediction up; below, down.

Latent variable scores​

The samples projected onto the first two latent variables, with a 95% Hotelling ellipse drawn around them.

The ellipse is the contour within which 95% of the samples should fall, if they all belonged to the same set:

(x−xˉ)⊤S−1(x−xˉ)≤χ0.95; 22(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) \leq \chi^{2}_{0.95;\,2}(x−xˉ)⊤S−1(x−xˉ)≤χ0.95;22​

It is not a circle because it accounts for covariance - if the two latent variables vary together, the ellipse comes out tilted.

What to look for: a sample outside the ellipse is an outlier candidate. Don't discard it automatically - go and check that sample's photo and ROI first. A good share of the points outside the ellipse are badly placed ROIs, not odd samples.

Explained variance​

Bars with the fraction each latent variable carries, plus a line with the running total.

It serves as a cross-check against the optimisation chart: if the first three variables already add up to 95% and the cross-validation error stops falling at the third, the two readings agree and the choice is well grounded.

RGB histogram per ROI​

One per region of interest. It shows, for each channel, how many pixels have each intensity.

This is the raw data everything comes from - in PLS it is literally the histogram that goes in as a variable. Worth a look whenever a result comes out strange:

  • A single narrow peak → homogeneous ROI. That is what you want.
  • Two peaks → the ROI is catching two things (sample and background, for instance).
  • A peak pressed against 255 → overexposed region. The information was lost at capture and no correction brings it back.

More on this in Regions of interest and in Quality charts.

Tables​

Alongside the charts come two tables:

  • Equation and ANOVA - the coefficients of the line and the test that says whether the slope is significant. A slope indistinguishable from zero means colour does not respond to concentration.
  • Sample predictions - each sample's estimated value with its uncertainty, in the form value ± half-width.
